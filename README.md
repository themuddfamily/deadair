# DEAD AIR

**Genre:** Psychological / supernatural horror  
**Perspective:** First-person  
**Setting:** Isolated emergency radio relay station, late 1990s or early 2000s  
**Structure:** Mostly single-location, narrative-driven, multiple endings  
**Combat:** Essentially none  
**Main mechanic:** Receiving transmissions from possible futures and deciding whether to interfere with them

The central idea is:

> **You can hear the future, but listening to it changes what happens.**

And eventually:

> **Something has realized you can hear it.**

---

# The opening

The game begins completely normally.

You're driving through heavy rain at night toward **Relay Station 14**, a small government communications facility several miles from the nearest town.

You aren't some paranormal investigator.

You're just an employee covering somebody else's night shift.

The station exists to receive emergency transmissions in an area where mountainous terrain makes normal radio communication unreliable.

The previous operator leaves almost immediately after you arrive.

He gives you a quick tour.

Generator room.

Equipment rack.

Kitchen.

Bathroom.

Maintenance room.

Emergency transmitter.

Backup antenna.

Then, almost as an afterthought:

> “Frequency 148.32 is reserved. If you hear anything on it, just log it. Don't respond.”

You ask why.

He shrugs.

> “Old military frequency or something.”

Then he drives away.

For the first twenty minutes or so, the game deliberately lets you **actually do the job**.

You make coffee.

Check the weather instruments.

Log a transmission from a truck driver.

Reset a circuit breaker.

Listen to boring police chatter.

Fill out paperwork.

The player learns the radio system without realizing they're essentially completing a tutorial.

Then at **02:13**, the radio crackles.

A terrified woman speaks.

> “Hello? Is anybody there?”

You respond.

She's trapped in a crashed car.

She gives you a road.

You locate it on the map.

She describes hitting a deer and rolling into a ditch.

You radio emergency services.

Everything seems normal.

Until the dispatcher responds:

> “There's nothing there.”

You check the woman's transmission log.

The receiver automatically timestamps everything.

Her transmission reads:

**28 OCT 1999 – 02:13**

But the date on your desk calendar is:

**27 OCT 1999.**

---

# The first major hook

The next morning doesn't arrive.

Not literally.

Time still passes.

But the night seems unnaturally long.

The clock moves normally, yet sunrise keeps getting delayed.

03:00.

04:00.

05:00.

Still pitch black.

Meanwhile, you start receiving more transmissions.

Most are completely mundane.

A truck breaking down.

Somebody calling for an ambulance.

A police pursuit.

A child reporting a fire.

But the timestamps vary.

Some are several minutes ahead.

Some hours.

Some days.

One transmission is dated **three years from now**.

Initially the player thinks:

> “Okay. I can prevent these things.”

And sometimes they can.

That becomes incredibly important because the game should deliberately reward the player early on for interfering.

You hear about an accident.

You warn somebody.

The accident doesn't happen.

Later you receive confirmation.

**You saved them.**

That teaches the player:

**Future transmissions can be changed.**

Which makes what comes later much worse.

# The station itself

The entire building should be small enough that the player learns it intimately.

By the end of the game, you should know every cupboard, every fluorescent light and every creaky floorboard.

I'd have roughly:

- Radio room
- Office
- Break room/kitchen
- Equipment/server room
- Generator basement
- Storage room
- Bathroom
- Maintenance workshop
- Locked records room
- Roof access
- Underground antenna control room
- A sealed door labelled **RELAY CHAMBER**

That last room initially isn't mentioned.

You might walk past it dozens of times.

It's just an old steel door tucked behind some equipment.

No objective points toward it.

Eventually the player notices something strange.

There is **no room behind it** according to the station blueprint.

---

# The radio system

The radio shouldn't just be a menu.

It should feel like physical equipment.

You're sitting at a desk with several analogue receivers.

Big tuning dial.

Signal-strength meter.

Spectrum display.

Cassette recorder.

Frequency notebook.

Microphone.

Directional antenna controls.

The player gradually becomes competent at operating it.

You might hear a faint voice buried underneath static.

You adjust:

**Frequency**

148.28  
148.29  
148.30

The voice becomes clearer.

Then you adjust the antenna orientation.

Northwest.

West.

Southwest.

Signal strength increases.

Eventually you isolate the transmission.

There could also be frequencies that behave very differently.

Emergency services.

Civilian CB.

Military.

Weather.

Unknown.

And gradually you discover frequencies that **shouldn't exist**.

For instance:

### 000.00 MHz

Complete silence.

Then breathing.

And somehow the breathing matches your own.

---

# Future delay mechanic

One of the coolest systems could be something called **Temporal Offset**.

Eventually the equipment starts displaying how far ahead a transmission originates.

For example:

**OFFSET: +00:00:07**

Seven seconds.

You hear:

> *chair scraping*

Seven seconds later, your chair moves slightly.

Then:

> *glass breaking*

Seven seconds later, something falls from a shelf.

The player quickly learns:

**The radio is predicting the room.**

This becomes gameplay.

Imagine you're hiding in the radio room.

Temporal offset:

**+00:00:12**

You hear footsteps.

You count.

Twelve seconds later:

Footsteps outside.

You hear the door handle turn through the radio.

Meaning you have twelve seconds to react.

Later the offset starts becoming unreliable.

+10 seconds.

+6 minutes.

-3 seconds.

Then:

**OFFSET: -00:00:04**

You're receiving something that already happened.

But you didn't hear it happen.

That implies there are events occurring inside the station that you somehow aren't perceiving.

---

# The first appearance of “you”

About 90 minutes into the game, you pick up a transmission.

Weak signal.

You tune it.

A man's voice.

Panicked.

> “Station Fourteen, listen carefully.”

There's something unsettlingly familiar about the voice.

> “Do not answer transmission 19.”

The player checks the incoming log.

They're currently on transmission 17.

The voice continues.

> “And whatever happens, don't unlock the records room.”

Then the transmission ends.

Later you hear a recording of yourself speaking.

Same voice.

Same mannerisms.

It's you.

The player realizes they're receiving communications from **their own future**.

---

# Transmission 19

Obviously this needs to be horrible.

Transmission 18 comes and goes.

Then the receiver automatically starts scanning.

148.32 MHz.

Transmission 19.

Silence.

Then:

> “Hello?”

A little girl's voice.

> “My dad told me somebody would be listening.”

She gives your name.

Not your character's job title.

Your actual name.

> “He said you're supposed to open the records room.”

Now the game lets you choose.

Open it.

Ignore her.

Question her.

Turn off the receiver.

Respond.

The choice doesn't create an immediate obvious consequence.

That's important.

The player shouldn't know what the correct decision was.

---

# The records room

If opened, the room contains decades of archived tapes.

The facility is much older than you were told.

Relay Station 14 supposedly opened in 1978.

But there are recordings dated:

**1954**

**1932**

**1901**

Eventually:

**1887**

Obviously radio didn't exist here in 1887.

One tape is simply labelled:

**FIRST CONTACT**

Another:

**DO NOT DUPLICATE**

Another:

**ADAM**

Or whatever the player character is called.

Which was recorded decades before they were born.

---

# The central mystery

The station was never really built to relay normal radio signals.

That is just its cover purpose.

In the 1950s, researchers discovered an unusual electromagnetic phenomenon beneath the mountain.

Signals transmitted through the geology occasionally arrived **before they were sent**.

Initially the effect was microscopic.

Milliseconds.

Scientists thought they had discovered some bizarre propagation phenomenon.

Then they constructed a massive underground antenna.

The delay increased.

Seconds.

Minutes.

Hours.

Years.

Eventually they realized the signal wasn't literally travelling backward in time.

Something much stranger was happening.

The receiver was picking up signals from **possible versions of the future**.

Not one future.

Thousands.

Every decision branches them.

The station effectively acts like a radio between realities.

---

# Why the station still exists

The government experiment was abandoned after something happened during testing.

Researchers began receiving transmissions from themselves.

Then from researchers who had died.

Then from versions of themselves claiming that their timelines had been destroyed.

Eventually they started receiving one repeating transmission.

Nobody could identify the speaker.

It always said:

> “KEEP THE CHANNEL OPEN.”

The project was terminated.

Except somebody discovered something terrifying.

Turning the equipment off caused catastrophic events around the station.

Disappearances.

Mass animal deaths.

People reporting missing hours.

So instead of destroying the station, they left it running.

Ever since then, somebody has been stationed there every night.

The operators aren't really monitoring emergencies.

Their actual job is simply:

**Keep the receiver occupied.**

---

# The entity

I'd deliberately avoid showing a conventional monster for most of the game.

The antagonist isn't really a creature walking around.

It's something living **between possible realities**.

Humans accidentally created a way for it to perceive us.

Normally it cannot enter a stable timeline.

But every time the player changes the future based on a transmission, they create another branch.

And every branch gives the entity another route closer.

So ironically:

**The more people you save, the stronger it becomes.**

That creates a nasty moral mechanic.

Ignore future distress calls?

People die.

Prevent them?

Something comes closer.

---

# How it manifests

Rather than appearing physically immediately, it begins creating inconsistencies.

You leave a mug beside the radio.

Later it's in the kitchen.

You distinctly remember shutting a door.

Now it's open.

A photograph on the wall contains three employees.

Later there are four.

You don't remember the fourth person.

Eventually you walk past a mirror and your reflection is delayed slightly.

Then the station layout begins subtly changing.

A corridor is longer.

A door leads somewhere different.

A room appears that you've never seen before.

Then you receive a radio transmission:

> “Do not go into the new hallway.”

Except you're already standing inside it.

---

# The camera system

There's an old security CCTV system.

Very low frame rate.

Maybe one frame per second.

Initially it is mundane.

Front entrance.

Generator.

Parking area.

Roof antenna.

Hallway.

But occasionally the cameras show things that aren't there.

You look at the hallway camera.

Someone is standing outside the radio room.

You turn around.

Nobody.

Look back at the monitor.

They're closer.

Turn around.

Nothing.

Monitor again.

They're now standing directly behind your chair.

But when you turn:

Still nothing.

Eventually you realize the cameras aren't showing the present.

Each camera has a slightly different temporal offset.

Camera 1: +8 seconds.

Camera 2: -2 minutes.

Camera 3: +19 years.

And camera 4 appears to show the station **after it has been abandoned**.

You can use this to solve puzzles.

---

# Phone calls

There should also be an old landline.

It almost never rings.

Which makes it terrifying when it does.

At one point:

**RING.**

You answer.

Nothing.

Then you hear yourself breathing.

You say:

> “Hello?”

And through the phone:

> “Hello?”

Exactly one second later.

It's repeating you.

You say something else.

It repeats it.

Then suddenly the voice says:

> “Don't turn around.”

Before you said anything.

---

# The other operators

Throughout the game you discover what happened to previous night operators.

Some survived.

Some disappeared.

Some apparently worked at the station for decades despite employment records saying otherwise.

One left hundreds of handwritten notes inside ventilation ducts.

Most are paranoid nonsense.

But one sentence appears repeatedly:

> DON'T TRUST THE VERSION OF YOU WHO KNOWS TOO MUCH.

Another operator recorded tapes addressed specifically to future employees.

On one:

> “If you start hearing your own voice, you're already part of the loop.”

Another:

> “Eventually one of you figures out how to survive. That's the dangerous one.”

That creates uncertainty around Future You.

---

# Multiple versions of the player

This should become the main narrative mechanic in the second half.

You begin receiving transmissions from several future versions of yourself.

They have different personalities because they've experienced different timelines.

For clarity, players might mentally categorize them.

**Calm You**

Very composed.

Seems knowledgeable.

Gives precise instructions.

> “At 04:47, disconnect antenna three.”

**Panicked You**

Terrified.

> “Don't listen to him. He isn't you anymore.”

**Injured You**

Constant coughing.

Claims something is inside the station.

**Old You**

Sounds decades older.

Knows things nobody else knows.

Then finally:

**You**

A voice coming through the radio in real time.

No offset.

Which should theoretically be impossible.

---

# The trust problem

The game should never give players a simple dialogue system where blue = good and red = evil.

Instead, you're constantly evaluating evidence.

For example:

Calm You says:

> “Open the basement ventilation duct.”

Panicked You says:

> “Don't.”

You investigate.

Inside the duct is a key.

Great.

Calm You was correct.

Later Calm You tells you:

> “Turn off the backup generator.”

You trust him.

The lights go out.

Something enters the station.

Now you're thinking:

Was he lying?

Or was turning off the generator necessary despite the consequence?

---

# The transmission log

The game automatically records everything.

At first it's a useful convenience.

Eventually it becomes horrifying because the log itself changes.

You remember:

**Transmission 26 – Woman trapped in elevator**

You check later.

It now says:

**Transmission 26 – NO TRANSMISSION**

Or:

**Transmission 26 – YOU SHOULD NOT REMEMBER THIS**

Eventually messages start appearing that were never broadcast.

**Transmission 41**

Sender:

**STATION 14**

Timestamp:

**NULL**

Message:

**I CAN HEAR YOU READING THIS.**

---

# Save system

You could even integrate saving into the horror.

The player uses a tape backup terminal.

Whenever they save:

**ARCHIVING CURRENT STATE**

Later you discover archived states aren't just save files.

They're timelines.

The station has been recording each version of events.

Potentially, loading a previous save could even produce tiny differences.

Not enough to screw with the player's progress unfairly.

Just unsettling details.

A chair moves.

Different weather.

A different photo.

One dialogue line changes.

The game never acknowledges it.

---

# The outside world

The player should technically be able to leave the station fairly early.

You walk outside.

Rain.

Forest.

Your car.

But if you drive away, something strange happens.

After several minutes:

You see lights ahead.

It's Relay Station 14.

You've somehow driven back to it.

You try another direction.

Same result.

Eventually the roads aren't even consistent.

You walk into the woods.

Ten minutes later you emerge through the station's **supply cupboard**.

No loading screen.

You just push past branches and suddenly shelves surround you.

At this point the player understands:

The station isn't simply experiencing strange events.

**Its spatial location is becoming unstable.**

---

# A particularly nasty sequence

I'd include a section where nothing visibly attacks you.

You're listening to a future feed from the radio room.

Offset:

**+00:03:14**

You hear yourself.

> “Okay. I'm leaving the station.”

Then footsteps.

Door opens.

Silence.

Then, faintly:

> “Oh God.”

Running.

Something impacts the front door.

Screaming.

Transmission ends.

You now know that approximately three minutes from now, you're apparently going to leave the station and something horrible will happen.

So naturally you decide:

**I'm not leaving.**

Three minutes pass.

Nothing happens.

You've changed the future.

Then a new transmission begins.

Offset:

**+00:00:20**

Your own voice:

> “Why didn't you leave?”

Ten seconds later:

> “It knows you heard the first one.”

Five seconds.

> “Don't look at the window.”

And then someone knocks on the window.

---

# The creature finally becoming physical

For most of the game, you never clearly see it.

When you finally do, it shouldn't look like some standard zombie.

I'd make its appearance depend on the instability of reality.

It looks vaguely humanoid but seems to occupy several positions simultaneously.

Its limbs aren't stretched or monstrous.

They're simply **inconsistent**.

One frame its arm is down.

Next frame its hand is on the wall.

Next frame it's three metres closer.

Not teleporting.

More like you're seeing several possible locations at once.

Its face might resemble people you've heard on the radio.

Sometimes your own.

This thing isn't mimicking people.

It contains **versions of them**.

---

# Death mechanic

If the creature catches you, instead of a typical death screen, the screen cuts to radio static.

Then you hear:

> “Station Fourteen? Is anybody there?”

A new operator answers.

You realize your death just became one of the transmissions another timeline will receive.

Then:

**LOAD ARCHIVE**

Meaning every player death is canon.

Your failed attempt exists somewhere in the system.

That could allow the game to occasionally use previous deaths.

Perhaps later you hear the exact audio from your earlier failed attempt.

---

# The halfway revelation

At around the midpoint, you finally access the underground antenna chamber.

It's enormous.

Far larger than the building could physically accommodate.

Miles of cables disappear into darkness.

And at the centre is an antenna structure pointing **downward**.

Not upward.

You discover the station isn't receiving something from space.

It's receiving something below.

Except geological surveys show nothing underneath except solid bedrock.

Then you find one final research report.

The scientists discovered the temporal effect wasn't generated by the antenna.

The antenna merely amplified something that was already there.

Something beneath the site had been **broadcasting long before humanity invented radio**.

---

# The big twist

Eventually Old You explains what's actually happening.

You were never randomly assigned this shift.

Every operator was specifically chosen.

Because everyone assigned to Station 14 eventually turns out to have been heard on recordings made **before they were born**.

The station essentially predicts who will work there.

The organization maintaining the station doesn't choose employees.

They listen to the tapes.

Then make sure those people eventually arrive.

Meaning your entire life may have been subtly manipulated to put you in this room tonight.

---

# Final act

Eventually the timelines begin collapsing together.

You see multiple versions of rooms overlapping.

The radio receives hundreds of transmissions simultaneously.

Your own voice fills every frequency.

Some versions of you died.

Some escaped.

Some destroyed the station.

Some apparently joined the thing.

You eventually discover three broad ways the situation might end.

### Destroy the antenna

You overload the underground array.

Future transmissions disappear.

The station starts collapsing.

You escape.

Months later, life appears normal.

Then while driving one day, your car radio crackles.

A voice says:

> “You only destroyed yours.”

Cut to black.

### Maintain the loop

You realize the station must continue operating.

You survive until sunrise.

A replacement operator arrives.

Exactly like you did at the beginning.

You give them the tour.

As you're about to leave you say:

> “Frequency 148.32 is reserved. If you hear anything on it, just log it. Don't respond.”

You suddenly realize:

**That's exactly what the previous operator told you.**

Maybe he wasn't being dismissive.

Maybe he was desperately trying to stop you.

### Open the channel

You decide Future You is correct.

Instead of closing the temporal phenomenon, you amplify it.

Every timeline briefly becomes connected.

The game shows dozens of versions of the radio room simultaneously.

Then everything becomes silent.

You wake up in daylight.

The station is abandoned.

Dust everywhere.

Your car has rusted away.

You find a newspaper.

The date is **27 October 2049.**

The radio crackles.

Your own voice from the beginning of the game:

> “Station Fourteen, radio check.”

You sit down.

Pick up the microphone.

And respond.

# Secret ending

I'd absolutely hide one ending that's incredibly difficult to discover.

Throughout the entire game, players assume they need to interact with the transmissions.

But technically…

they don't.

If the player never responds to **any future transmission**, never opens the records room, never follows Future You and performs only their ordinary job responsibilities, eventually morning arrives.

06:42.

Sunrise.

Nothing happens.

Your replacement arrives.

You leave.

Credits.

Except after the credits:

A recording plays.

Researchers discussing your shift.

> “Subject demonstrated zero interaction.”

Another voice:

> “So that's it?”

> “Yes.”

Pause.

> “Without observation, there was no event.”

Meaning the whole catastrophe occurs because the player investigates it.

And almost nobody will get that ending on their first playthrough because **who the hell plays a horror game and ignores the creepy radio?**

# Audio would be ridiculously important

I think this game would live or die on sound.

I'd almost avoid music completely for long stretches.

You'd hear:

rain against windows,

electrical hum,

fluorescent lights,

radio static,

distant thunder,

pipes expanding,

wind against the antenna tower.

After an hour, players become intimately familiar with those sounds.

So when something changes, they immediately notice.

Maybe the generator normally makes:

**rrrrrrrrrrrrrrr**

Then suddenly:

**rrrrrrr—rrr—rrrrr**

And your brain immediately goes:

*"That's not normal."*

Or you hear footsteps upstairs.

Except you already know from exploring that the station only has **one floor**.

**DO NOT INVESTIGATE BACKUP GENERATOR.**

**You already investigated it.**

Nothing else.

*knock.*

*knock.*

*knock.*

# Why I think the concept works

The radio gives the game an excuse to constantly tell the player terrifying things **without immediately showing them**.

That's hugely effective for horror because the player's imagination does most of the work.

You could tell someone:

> “At 03:47 something will enter the building.”

And then let them spend fifteen real minutes wondering what the hell that means.

Every tiny noise becomes terrifying.

The player starts checking clocks.

Locking doors.

Watching cameras.

Trying to decide whether the warning itself is what causes the event.

And the most important thing would be that the game **never establishes a completely reliable source of information**.

The radio isn't lying.

The future versions of you aren't necessarily lying.

The previous operators aren't necessarily lying.

They're simply describing **different futures**.

So two completely contradictory warnings can both be true.

That's what I'd make the defining idea of **Dead Air**:

> **The future isn't predetermined. But every time you look at it, you make one version of it more real.**
